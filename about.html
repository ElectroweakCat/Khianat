What is Khianat?

Khianat is my self-made chess engine. It runs completely in your browser: there is no engine server, no cloud, nothing phones home when you play. Everything you see on the Play page is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

This page explains how the engine actually works, in the same order in which Khianat itself thinks about a move. All descriptions match the code that is really running on this site.

Why "Khianat"?

When I started this project I happened to be learning Indonesian, and I was looking for a name that you would not find on any other chess site. That is how I stumbled over the word khianat: it means betrayal, treachery. It is one of the many Arabic loanwords in Indonesian, going back to Arabic خِيَانَة (khiyāna), which travelled through Classical Malay into modern Indonesian and, along other routes, into Persian, Turkish, Hindi and several more languages.

I liked the sound of it, and the meaning fits a chess engine that is supposed to win with tactics, traps and moves you did not see coming. The logo tells the same story: a humble pawn casting the shadow of a queen. It looks harmless. It is not.

Architecture

The project is split into small parts, each with one job:

chess.js (by Jeff Hlywa) implements the rules of chess: it knows which moves are legal in a position, detects check, checkmate, stalemate and draws, and can play moves forward and take them back. Khianat never invents moves itself, it only chooses among the legal moves this library generates. Illegal moves are impossible by construction.

chessboard.js (by Chris Oakman) draws the board and handles the dragging of pieces. It is pure presentation and knows nothing about the rules.

evaluation.js is my evaluation function: it looks at a position and answers "who is better, and by how much?" with a single number.

engine.js is the brain: the opening book and the search algorithm that decides which move to play.

The evaluation function

At the bottom of every calculation sits a simple question: is this position good or bad? Khianat answers it in centipawns (100 = one pawn) from White's point of view, using two ingredients:

Material. Every piece type has a fixed value: a knight is worth roughly three pawns, a queen roughly nine. The exact numbers are adapted from the Sunfish engine.

Piece square tables. The same piece is worth more on a good square than on a bad one. For every piece type there is an 8×8 table of bonuses and penalties. A knight in the center gets a bonus, a knight in the corner a hefty penalty. Black uses the same tables, mirrored vertically.

The evaluation walks over all 64 squares and adds everything up: White's material and square bonuses count positive, Black's count negative. Deliberately missing (so far): pawn structure, king safety and mobility.

The search: Minimax

A good evaluation of the current position is not enough, because chess is about what happens next. So Khianat builds a tree: my possible moves, all your answers to each of them, all my answers to those, and so on. At the leaves of the tree the evaluation function is called.

The values then flow back up by the Minimax rule: on my turns I pick the maximum (best for me), on your turns I assume you pick the minimum (worst for me). Khianat plays the move whose subtree survives this pessimistic calculation best. In the code this is written in the negamax form, which is the same algorithm with the sign flipped at every level, so one function handles both colors.

Iterative deepening. Khianat does not search one fixed depth. It searches depth 1, then depth 2, then depth 3 and so on until its time budget is used up, and plays the best move of the last fully completed depth. This has two nice effects: the thinking time is predictable, and in simple positions (especially endgames) the search automatically reaches much deeper. If only one legal move exists, it is played instantly.

Difficulty levels. The difficulty levels on the Play page simply control this search: lower levels cap the search depth and thinking time, the highest level is the unrestricted engine. Same brain, shorter or longer leash.

Alpha-beta pruning

The move tree grows brutally fast: with roughly 35 legal moves per position, four half-moves already mean over a million leaves. Alpha-beta pruning cuts this down without changing the result. The idea: while searching, remember the best result each side can already force (called alpha and beta). As soon as a branch is proven to be worse than something we already have, the rest of that branch cannot influence the final decision and is skipped.

Move ordering. Pruning works best when the strongest moves are searched first. Khianat therefore sorts moves before searching them: promotions and captures come first (preferring "small piece takes big piece"), and at the root the best move of the previous, shallower iteration is always tried first. Quiescence search

A fixed search depth has a famous blind spot called the horizon effect: if the search stops right after "queen takes pawn", the position looks great, even when the queen gets captured on the very next move, just beyond the horizon.

Khianat avoids this with a quiescence search: at the leaves of the tree the search does not simply stop, it keeps playing captures and promotions (only those) until the position is quiet, and evaluates then. The side to move may also "stand pat", i.e. decline all captures, so quiet positions stay cheap. As a result Khianat does not fall for the classic "grab a pawn, lose the queen" trap.

Opening, middlegame, endgame

Opening. For the first one or two moves Khianat plays its personal, hand-written opening book: a small table of positions with weighted replies (Sicilian, Modern defense, occasionally even the Englund gambit). Every book move is checked against the legal moves before it is played. The book is deliberately tiny; it is the engine's personality, not a database.

Middlegame. Here the search and the piece square tables do the work: pieces are pushed towards active squares, the king hides behind its pawns, captures are calculated to the end.

Endgame. When little non-pawn material is left on the board, the king switches to a separate endgame table: instead of hiding in the corner it walks to the center, which is exactly what you should do in a real endgame. Checkmate is scored as a huge value minus the distance to mate, so Khianat prefers the fastest mate and, when losing, drags things out as long as possible. Stalemate and drawn positions (fifty-move rule, threefold repetition, insufficient material) count as 0, so the engine will not accidentally stalemate you in a winning position, and it will happily steer into a draw when it is lost.

Performance and stability

The search runs in a Web Worker, a background thread, so the page stays responsive while Khianat thinks (if the browser does not allow a worker, the engine simply thinks in the foreground instead). Beyond that, the search has to be a bit careful:

The clock is only checked every so often, because reading the system time is surprisingly expensive. When the time is up, the search does not abort with an exception; it sets a flag and unwinds level by level, so every trial move is guaranteed to be taken back (a lesson learned the hard way, see blog entry #8). As a second safety net the engine remembers the length of the real game history before thinking and restores it afterwards, so the game state can never be corrupted by the search.

With all of this, Khianat at full strength searches several half-moves deep in the middlegame and clearly deeper in the endgame.

Playing strength

My honest estimate: around 1500 to 1700 Elo against human casual players at the highest difficulty. Khianat calculates short tactics reliably, does not hang pieces to simple threats and wins won positions cleanly. The lower difficulty levels scale this down to something a beginner can beat. Its known weaknesses: the evaluation knows nothing about pawn structure, king safety or mobility, there is no transposition table (the same position reached by different move orders is searched again), and quiet positional play beyond its search depth escapes it. Patient, solid play is the best recipe against it.

What could come next? A transposition table, pawn structure and king safety in the evaluation, and check evasions in the quiescence search. Each of these is a realistic future blog entry.
